What is DBT?

Dialectical Behavioral Therapy, or DBT, is based upon the foundation that two seemingly opposite ideas can be true. It was invented by Marsha Lineham in the 1980s as a subset of CBT, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, a therapy focused on the idea that thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected. The goal of DBT is to use skills to combat ineffective coping and create a life worth living. It is composed of four modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotional regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. The skills on this site are organized based on these four modules, but they are not limited to DBT skills (there are also some options from other therapy models!). There is also a section with neurodivergent-specific resources that helps explain how DBT skills can be modified to work for minds of all kinds.

Neurodivergence

Imagine you are taking a course in mathematics. As the professor details a new formula, you fill your notebook with detailed notes and pay close attention as the professor solves practice problems on the board. You leave the class feeling confident in your new skills. However, as you try to complete the homework, you are unable to solve any of the problems on your own. You go to different tutors, professors, and peers, but the cycle continues. Everyone tells you to try harder, to really absorb the lessons they are teaching you. Nobody considers the impact of their teaching method on your learning process - until someone does. Once someone finally explains the lesson to you differently, it all makes perfect sense

How does this story relate to neurodivergence?

I first learned DBT skills at 18 years old, at a point when I was really struggling with my mental health. In DBT groups/programs, I demonstrated a mastery of the skills. However, when it came to applying the skills in real life, I had little to no success. As my struggling continued, I was sent from one DBT program to another. After a difficult year in and out of DBT programs, I was diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder. My therapist explained that individuals on the spectrum can certainly benefit from DBT skills, but these skills often need to be modified and taught differently to be effective for autistic individuals. Within a year of recieving the diagnosis and modifying my treatment plan accordingly, my symptoms vastly improved and my life returned to some degree of normalcy
